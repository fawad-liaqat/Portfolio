# Pakistan built the rails. How complete is the stack?

**Part 1 of 4: an evaluation of Pakistan's digital public infrastructure against India, Brazil and Egypt**

Fawad Liaqat. Evidence as of 17 November 2026. Source markers such as [S2] refer to the source list at the end.

## The question

Pakistan's instant payment system, Raast, moved 2,755.2 million transactions in fiscal year 2026 [S2, p. 31]. Yet only 27 percent of Pakistani adults have a financial account and only 25 percent made or received a digital payment in the latest global survey [S4, p. 298]. That gap drives this series. Part 1 asks where Pakistan stands, and where it lacks, across the three layers of digital public infrastructure and the rules around them, compared with India, Brazil and Egypt. It also asks which lessons can travel here, given who Pakistanis are and what phones they hold.

## Why these three countries

I picked two benchmarks and one peer. India is the closest structural match: a large South Asian country that built identity, payments and consented data sharing as public systems under similar demographic limits [S10] [S8] [S11]. Brazil is the closest institutional match, because its central bank runs the instant payment rail and mandated open finance [S5, p. 93 and p. 103]. That is what the State Bank is doing with Raast and its open banking draft [S2, p. 14] [S3, p. 6]. Egypt is a realistic peer. It sits in the same lower middle income group, its account numbers are closer to ours, and the World Bank scores it on the same regional framework [S1, p. 11 and p. 57] [S4, p. 296].

## What DPI is

Digital public infrastructure (DPI) means the shared digital foundations that many services can reuse. The World Bank's 2026 regional assessment counts three: digital identity with electronic signatures, digital payments, and data sharing. Around them sits an enabling layer of laws, data protection and institutions [S1, p. 2 and p. 9]. If each exists once and they work together, a bank or a small shop does not have to rebuild them. I use that framework and its categorical style, with no numeric scores.

In short: Pakistan matches its peers on the payment rail, trails India and Brazil on digital ID and data sharing, and is last on data protection law. Underneath, far fewer Pakistanis hold an account.

## Layer by layer

### Identity

Pakistan's base is strong. Findex data reported by the World Bank puts ID ownership at 97 percent of men and 89 percent of women, an 8 point gap described as among the largest in the world [S1, p. 19].

The digital layer on top is not finished. The World Bank classes PAK ID as a pilot [S1, p. 16 and p. 57]. The report is not consistent here: another figure lists PAK ID with the remote authentication systems [S1, p. 17]. A March 2025 state news report calls the phone based card a planned addition to the Pak ID app [S19]. I treat it as a pilot.

India reported about 1.34 billion live Aadhaar holders and more than 170 billion cumulative authentications by March 2026 [S10]. The World Bank cites Aadhaar as having cut a bank's cost of an electronic identity check from USD 12 to USD 0.06 [S1, p. 75]. Brazil's gov.br login reached 175 million users in March 2026, with 83.4 million at the highest assurance tier [S14]. Egypt has near universal ID cards for men and women [S1, p. 19], but its digital ID, Haweya, is still under design [S1, p. 16].

### Payments

This is Pakistan's best layer. In FY26 (provisional) retail payments reached 14.3 billion transactions worth PKR 673 trillion, and digital channels carried over 92 percent of them by count [S2, p. 7]. Merchants with a QR code grew from 1,092,044 to 3,836,522 in a year, which the State Bank links to a subsidy under the Cashless Pakistan Initiative [S2, p. 7 and p. 9].

Four numbers temper this.

**Value.** Digital channels carried 38 percent of retail value [S2, p. 7]. By my calculation from the same table, about 62 percent (PKR 415.7 trillion of 672.7 trillion) still moved over the counter [S2, p. 9].

**Merchants.** Person to merchant payments on Raast rose from 4.9 million to 182.5 million [S2, p. 31]. That is still only 6.6 percent of Raast volume (my calculation: 182.5 of 2,755.2 million). In India, merchant payments are 63 percent of UPI volume, and 86 percent of them are under INR 500 [S9]. In Brazil, a press report of the central bank's Pix report puts payments from people to businesses at 43 percent [S17]. Across all channels, account based merchant payments were 13.0 percent of Pakistan's retail payments [S2, p. 19], so the direction holds either way.

**Intensity.** The State Bank reports 297 million QR and till payments against 3.8 million QR merchants [S2, p. 10]. My arithmetic puts that near 77 payments per merchant per year, about one and a half a week. Many merchants joined partway through the year, so the true rate is higher, but a code on the counter is not a code in use.

**People.** Account ownership is 27 percent in Pakistan, against 89 percent in India, 86 percent in Brazil and 43 percent in Egypt. Digital payment use is 25, 48, 77 and 36 percent [S4, pp. 295 to 298].

The 27 percent sits well below the financial inclusion rate the State Bank publishes, and the two should not be read side by side. Findex is a survey: it counts adults who say they personally hold an account at a bank, similar institution or mobile money provider [S4, p. 83]. The State Bank's National Financial Inclusion Strategy defines inclusion broadly as the share of adults with a bank account, with a baseline of 64 percent in December 2023 and a target of 75 percent by June 2028 [S21, p. 1 and p. 23]. The Governor's Annual Report for 2025-26 puts it at 69 percent, with the gender gap at 29 percent, and introduces a wider Pakistan Financial Inclusion Index that scores access, usage and quality separately [S22, p. 47 to 48]. Neither document says how the headline rate is measured; the strategy's earlier figures came from Access to Finance surveys, and it plans a new survey of that kind [S21, p. 4 and p. 19]. With different sources and dates, the two numbers are not comparable, and the published documents do not explain a gap this large.

Scale differs hugely. UPI processed about 241.6 billion transactions in FY 2025 to 26 [S8]. Pix processed 7.9 billion in December 2025 alone and is used by 172 million people and 22.2 million businesses [S5, p. 93]. Egypt's network had 16 million users and moved about EGP 4.2 trillion in the year to June 2025 [S6, p. 43].

### Data sharing

Here Pakistan has the least. The World Bank marks its national data sharing platform as absent and under development, with no enterprise service bus and no government API standards [S1, p. 40]. Egypt's government platform has linked about 100 public bodies since 2015 [S1, p. 43].

In finance, Pakistan is drafting. The State Bank's draft Open Banking Regulations 2026 would require every bank, microfinance bank and electronic money institution to open its APIs to licensed third parties, with Raast as a central API hub [S3, p. 5 and p. 6]. Three firms are testing open banking in the regulatory sandbox [S2, p. 20]. India's Account Aggregator network had fulfilled 595.11 million consents across 352.19 million linked accounts by 30 September 2026 [S11]. Brazil's Open Finance had connected 96 million accounts with more than 154 million active consents by the end of 2025 [S5, p. 103]. Egypt's open banking rules appear to be in development, per one undated tracker [S20].

### Enablers

Pakistan is one of seven countries in the World Bank's regional sample with no data protection law. The bill is a draft [S1, p. 46 and p. 47]. It has a cybersecurity law (2016) and a data classification framework, but no interoperability framework [S1, p. 46].

Brazil's 2018 law has applied since September 2020, and its regulator can inspect and sanction [S15] [S16]. India enacted its act in August 2023 and notified rules in November 2025 with an eighteen month phase in [S13]. Egypt passed a law in 2020, but its regulator is not yet operational [S1, p. 46 and p. 48].

## Where Pakistan lacks, ranked

1. **Too few people hold an account.** 27 percent of adults, with a 30 point gap between men and women [S4, p. 298 and p. 98]. The rail cannot serve people who are not on it.
2. **Merchant payments are thin.** 6.6 percent of Raast volume [S2, p. 31], against 63 percent on UPI [S9].
3. **No data protection law.** Pakistan is the only one of the four with nothing enacted [S1, p. 46]. Open banking is being drafted on sector circulars [S3, p. 11].
4. **The data sharing layer is missing.** No government platform, no interoperability framework, and financial data sharing is a draft [S1, p. 40 and p. 46; S3, p. 5].
5. **Digital ID is a pilot.** The card is nearly universal, but reusable remote login is not live at scale [S1, p. 57].
6. **Women and basic phones are an afterthought in design.** Findex reports a 65 point gap in phone ownership between Pakistani men and women, and counts Pakistan among countries with large shares of basic phone owners [S4, p. 20 and p. 25]. GSMA's 2024 survey shows a smaller gap, 93 percent of men against 58 percent of women [S7, p. 25]. The surveys disagree on size, not direction. Meanwhile the draft open banking consent journey runs through app dashboards, one time passwords and app PINs [S3, p. 7 and p. 8].

## Lessons that transfer, and lessons that do not

**Small merchant payments can become the main use (India, Brazil).** Most UPI payments go to merchants and most of those are small [S9]. In Brazil, 71 percent of Pix transactions were BRL 100 or less [S17]. What helps: Raast merchant payments grew 37 times in a year (my calculation) after a subsidy and QR rollout [S2, p. 31]. What blocks: low use per merchant, and no primary source on why merchants there adopted. Confidence: medium on the direction, low on the mechanism.

**Accounts do not guarantee use (India).** India has 89 percent account ownership but 48 percent digital payment use, and 16 percent of account owners hold only inactive accounts [S4, p. 297 and p. 197]. Pakistan is the reverse: few accounts, but about 90 percent of owners pay digitally [S4, p. 167]. An account drive alone would not finish the job. Confidence: medium high.

**A channel for basic phones (India).** The Reserve Bank of India launched UPI123Pay in 2022 for what it counted as more than 400 million feature phone subscribers [S12]. What helps: 46.1 million call centre and IVR banking users here [S2, p. 9]. What blocks: no official usage data [S9]. Confidence: medium that the need transfers, low that the product does.

**Consent based data sharing at scale (India, Brazil).** Both countries show it can reach hundreds of millions of consents [S11; S5, p. 103]. What helps: the State Bank's draft has explicit, time bound, revocable consent and no hub charge for third parties [S3, p. 7 and p. 12]. What blocks: no data protection law [S1, p. 46], a 27 percent account base [S4, p. 298], and an app heavy journey [S3, p. 7]. Confidence: medium low.

**A law is not a regulator (Egypt).** Egypt passed a law in 2020 and its authority is still not operational [S1, p. 48]. This transfers fully as a warning. Confidence: high.

**ID pays off when services reuse it (India, Brazil).** Cheaper identity checks and one login for many services are the returns [S1, p. 75; S14]. What helps: NADRA's coverage and its biometric checks, already written into the draft open banking flow [S3, p. 7]. What blocks: the ID gender gap [S1, p. 19]. Confidence: medium.

**What does not transfer.** Scale does not. India and Brazil built on populations where close to nine in ten adults already held accounts [S4, p. 265]. Brazil's newest Pix feature needs a smartphone with NFC and internet [S5, p. 93]. A lesson that assumes an account and a smartphone mostly reaches people already served.

## What this means for adoption

The rail is not the bottleneck. The evidence points to access before use: most adults have no account, and those who do already pay digitally [S4, p. 167]. So Part 2 will ask why nearly three in four adults stay outside [S4, p. 298]. Candidates: design for the phones people own, cost, trust, acceptance, useful things to pay for, family permission, and financial literacy.

Literacy is a hypothesis, not a finding. Two facts keep it on the list. Findex reports difficulty reading or typing as a common reason for not owning a phone in South Asia [S4, p. 32]. The 2023 census puts literacy at 68.00 percent for men and 52.84 percent for women, and 41.67 percent for rural women [S18]. But the same Findex page reports that 40 percent of Pakistani women without a phone cite family disapproval [S4, p. 32]. It is one enabler among several, of unknown weight.

## Limits of this analysis

- SBP FY26 figures are provisional [S2]. Years differ: FY26 for Pakistan [S2], April 2025 to March 2026 for India [S8], calendar 2025 for Brazil [S5], the year to June 2025 for Egypt [S6]. Findex figures are from a 2024 survey [S4].
- The Pix annual total and merchant share are from the press [S17].
- Findex and GSMA disagree on the phone gender gap, and the GSMA values come from a chart [S7].
- Findex prints Pakistan's account ownership as 27 percent in its annex and 23 percent in its text [S4, p. 298 and p. 167]. I use the annex.
- Egypt's central bank reports 76.3 percent inclusion from its own database [S6, p. 47], against 43 percent in Findex [S4, p. 296]. I use Findex.
- India and Brazil are outside the World Bank regional report, so their cells rest on several sources and some are not found. Statuses are my reading of the evidence.

I would value feedback and other readings of the data.
