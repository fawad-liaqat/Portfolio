# What is digital public infrastructure, and how is Pakistan doing?

Think of roads. A country builds them once, and buses, ambulances and vans all use them. Digital public infrastructure, or DPI, is the same idea for the digital world. It is a small set of shared systems that banks, government offices and businesses can all build on.

The World Bank describes three layers, plus the rules around them [S1].

Why India, Brazil and Egypt? India and Brazil are the two closest models to learn from, and Egypt is a peer at a similar stage [S1] [S4].

**1. Identity: proving who you are.**
Everyday example: you open a mobile wallet and the agent checks your thumbprint against NADRA's records. Pakistan is strong on the basic ID card. About 97 in 100 men and 89 in 100 women have one [S1]. The newer step, logging in to services from your phone with a digital ID, is still at the pilot stage [S1].

**2. Payments: moving money instantly.**
Everyday example: you scan a QR code at a shop and the shopkeeper gets the money in seconds through Raast. This is Pakistan's best layer. Raast carried about 2.76 billion payments last fiscal year, and the number of shops with a QR code more than tripled to 3.8 million [S2].

**3. Data sharing: letting your records travel, with your permission.**
Everyday example: you apply for a small loan and, instead of collecting stamped bank statements, you tap "allow" and the lender sees six months of your account history. Pakistan is not there yet. The State Bank has published draft rules for this, called open banking, and a few firms are testing it [S3] [S2].

**The rules around it.**
Everyday example: a company misuses your phone number and you want someone to complain to. That needs a data protection law and an office that enforces it. Pakistan's law is still a draft [S1].

## So how is Pakistan doing?

Next to India, Brazil and Egypt, Pakistan's payment rail holds up well. It trails India and Brazil on digital ID and data sharing, and it is the only one of the four without a data protection law on the books [S1] [S13] [S15].

The bigger issue is who is on the rail. Only 27 in 100 Pakistani adults have any financial account. In India it is 89 and in Brazil 86 [S4]. Women are further behind: surveys agree that far fewer women than men own a phone, though they disagree on how wide the gap is [S4] [S7].

Pakistan has mostly built these systems. The open question is why so many people are still outside them. The phone they own, cost, trust, places to pay, family permission or know how? Part 2 looks into that.
