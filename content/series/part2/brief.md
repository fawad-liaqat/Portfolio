# The adoption gap: who is outside, and why

**Part 2 of 4: Pakistan's digital public infrastructure series**

Fawad Liaqat. Evidence as of November 2026. Source markers such as [S1] refer to the source list at the end. The tables behind the charts follow the same source list.

## The question

Part 1 found that the payment rail is not the bottleneck. Raast moved 2,755.2 million transactions in FY26 [S2, p. 31], yet only 27 percent of Pakistani adults have a financial account and 25 percent made or received a digital payment [S1, p. 298]. This part asks who is outside the system and what keeps them out, then what keeps cash in the hands of people already inside. It tests one popular explanation, financial literacy, against the data.

## Four numbers that measure different things

Readers will meet very different inclusion figures for Pakistan, so it helps to separate them first.

| Measure | Value | What it counts |
|---|---|---|
| Global Findex 2025 | 27% of adults [S1, p. 298] | Adults 15+ who say they personally hold an account at a bank, similar institution or mobile money provider [S1, p. 83] |
| Karandaaz K-FIS 2024 | 35% of adults; men 56%, women 14% [S12, p. 7] | Adults 15+ with an account in their own name at a regulated bank, mobile money provider or nonbank financial institution [S12, p. 3]; 6,722 interviews [S12, p. 5] |
| SBP, 2025 | 69% of adults; gender gap 29% [S11, pp. 47 and 48] | Broadly, the share of adults with a bank account, from SBP data [S11, p. 47] |

The State Bank's new Financial Inclusion Index is a different kind of number again: a score of 59.5 for 2025, built from 69 indicators. Its access score is 74.1, usage 68.5 and quality 43.5 [S11, p. 48]. Quality is the weakest part. Survey measures count people. Administrative measures count accounts, and Pakistan has 292.8 million customer accounts and wallets [S2, p. 10] against a population of 241.5 million [S2, p. 9]. One person can hold several. SBP's figure rose from 64 percent in 2023 against a target of 75 percent by 2028 [S15, pp. 1 and 23]. I use Findex for comparisons with other countries and Karandaaz, where readable, for detail inside Pakistan.

## Part A: access. Who is outside?

### Women

Every source agrees on direction. Findex reports a 30 point gender gap in account ownership, four or more times the global gap [S1, p. 98]. Karandaaz finds 14 percent of women included against 56 percent of men, a 42 point gap [S12, p. 7]. Even the State Bank, whose measure gives higher numbers, reports a 29 percent gap for 2025 [S11, p. 48]; the Governor put women's inclusion at 52 percent in December 2025 [S10].

The gap starts before the bank. Findex puts the gap in phone ownership between Pakistani men and women at 65 points, far above the 14 to 25 points it reports for Bangladesh, Egypt, Ethiopia and India [S1, p. 25]. Only 5 percent of men lack a phone [S1, p. 32]. Karandaaz reports that 47 percent of women own a SIM card, against 78 percent of men, the widest gender gap among its readiness indicators [S12, p. 10]. Branchless banking app users (99.1 million) far outnumber bank app users (30.4 million) [S2, p. 9], so a woman without her own SIM is outside the busiest entry door.

### People outside the labour force

Pakistan has the largest gap in South Asia between adults in and out of the labour force, at 29 points [S1, p. 101]. Without a wage paid into an account, there is less reason to open one.

### Poorer and less educated adults

Findex gives these only by region: 71 percent of South Asian adults without an account have a primary education or less [S1, p. 110]. Less educated adults are also more likely to be poor, so the two cannot be read apart [S1, p. 101].

### Teenagers

A new State Bank framework lets people aged 13 to 18 own and operate accounts and wallets on their own, closing what SBP calls a longstanding gap for about 26 million people [S11, p. 48]. It is too new to measure.

### Rural adults

In the 2023 census, 51.56 percent of rural people aged 10 and over are literate, against 74.09 percent in cities. The widest gap is between urban men at 78.13 percent and rural women at 41.67 percent, 36.5 points apart (my calculation) [S6]. Karandaaz finds the same divide in finance: 19 percent of rural adults pass its financial literacy test, against 27 percent in cities [S12, p. 11]. Across low and middle income economies, rural adults are 16 points less likely to pay digitally [S1, p. 171].

### What the unbanked say

Findex asked adults without a bank account why. In South Asia, excluding India, 70 percent said they do not have enough money, 36 percent said fees are too high, 24 percent said banks are too far, 20 percent lack documents, 17 percent said a family member has one and 15 percent do not trust institutions [S1, p. 111]. Respondents could pick several; Pakistan's own row is not printed.

## Part B: use. Why does cash persist for people already inside?

Here the Pakistan data turns a common story around. Among Pakistani account owners, 90 percent or more use digital payments, in both poorer and wealthier households [S1, p. 171]. India's figure is 54 percent [S1, p. 167]. Findex suggests social transfers into accounts may drive Pakistan's [S1, p. 171]. BISP serves more than 10 million beneficiaries, mostly women, and the State Bank has issued instructions to move them to Social Protection Wallets [S11, p. 57]. So most people inside already pay digitally. The problem is what they pay for and how often.

**Cash still carries value.** Over the counter transactions stayed above 60 percent of retail value in FY26 [S11, p. 21]; my calculation from the review gives about 62 percent (PKR 415.7 of 672.7 trillion) [S2, p. 9]. Currency in circulation grew from PKR 10,634.5 billion to 11,941.5 billion, about 12 percent (my calculation) [S2, p. 9]. Nearly 98 percent of cash deposits are still made at branches and agents [S2, p. 19].

**Acceptance is wide but thin, and it was paid for.** Pakistan has 3.8 million QR merchants, but they took 297 million QR and till payments in a year [S2, pp. 9 and 10], about 77 each (my calculation). Of 3.63 million Raast registered merchants, a separate count, 2.68 million joined in FY26 [S11, p. 58]. A federal subsidy of PKR 3.5 billion paid 0.5 percent of each Raast merchant payment, capped at PKR 100, from 1 September 2025 to 30 June 2026 [S11, pp. 21 and 58]. Monthly merchant payments rose from 4.3 million to 86 million over that period [S11, p. 21]. That June figure is about 24 payments per registered Raast merchant per month (my calculation, 86 million over 3.63 million [S11, p. 58]). On 13 November 2026 the government said it would continue subsidising merchant fees on Raast QR payments; the terms were not published [S13]. Whether use holds unaided is the test. Merchant payments are 6.6 percent of Raast volume (my calculation from [S2, p. 31]). In India they are 63 percent of UPI volume, and 86 percent of those are under INR 500 [S8]. 

**Cost and trust.** Fees are the second most cited barrier to accounts in South Asia [S1, p. 111]. On trust, 15 percent of the unbanked in the region cite it [S1, p. 111], and Pakistan still has no data protection law [S3, p. 46]. Findex warns that adults with little schooling may be more exposed to fraud, so protection has to be designed for them [S1, p. 110]. I found no Pakistan specific survey data on fraud among users.

**Design.** Pakistan has a relatively large share of basic phone owners [S1, p. 20]. India built a channel for them, UPI123Pay [S14]. The State Bank's open banking draft builds its consent journey around app dashboards and app PINs [S4, p. 7].

**Women inside the system.** Findex prints 74 percent of Pakistani women account owners using digital payments, against 95 percent of men [S1, p. 170]. The figure title on the same page says no gap is present in Pakistan, so I treat this one as unsettled.

## Part C: testing the financial literacy hypothesis

The claim worth testing is simple: people stay out, or stay in cash, because they do not understand financial services. Here is what the evidence I could read says.

**Evidence that supports a literacy role.** For Pakistani men and women who know about mobile internet but do not use it, difficulty reading or writing is the top single reason in GSMA's 2024 survey [S5, pp. 66 to 67]. Findex lists difficulty reading or typing as a common barrier to phone ownership in South Asia [S1, p. 32]. South Asian women without a bank account are at least 19 points more likely than men to say they would need help using one [S1, p. 246]. On Karandaaz's digital literacy index, women score 48 against 65 for men, and rural adults 51 against 70 in cities [S12, p. 30]. Basic literacy is lower for women too: 52.84 percent against 68.00 percent of men in the census, 15 points apart (my calculation from [S6]).

**A direct measure, and what it shows.** Karandaaz tests financial literacy directly, with questions on risk diversification, inflation, percentages and interest compounding [S12, p. 11]. In 2024, 22 percent of adults passed, up from 16 percent in 2016 [S12, p. 10]. The gaps follow the exclusion gaps closely: men 32 percent and women 12 percent, urban 27 and rural 19, adults with no formal education 7 and those with higher education 42 [S12, p. 11]. Among the financially included 38 percent pass, against 12 percent of the excluded [S12, p. 11]. Its 0 to 100 index tells the same story: included adults score 70 and excluded adults 48 [S12, p. 30].

**Evidence that points elsewhere.** Lack of money and fees top the barrier list [S1, p. 111]. Family disapproval is cited by 40 percent of Pakistani women without a phone, and by no men [S1, p. 32]. And once inside, 90 percent or more of Pakistani account owners use digital payments regardless of income [S1, p. 171]. Findex itself warns that financial education helps but is not enough alone; many problems come from pricing, design and opacity, not user mistakes [S1, p. 239].

**What policy is already doing.** The State Bank's financial education roadmap reached 2.6 million people in FY26, and the school curriculum project has finalised 192 learning outcomes for grades 1 to 12 [S11, p. 48]. SBP credits financial literacy, among other things, for rising index scores [S11, p. 48]. That is an assessment, not a test.

**What the data cannot settle.** The link is strong, but it is an association, not proof of cause. Using an account may itself teach people these ideas. Education, income and gender norms may drive both literacy and inclusion. And other readiness gaps are wider: 96 percent of included adults own a SIM against 44 percent of the excluded [S12, p. 11], a 52 point gap against 26 points for financial literacy (my calculation). No study I read tests whether teaching financial literacy raises account use in Pakistan. The State Bank plans its own demand side survey to capture literacy levels [S15, p. 19].

**My reading.** Financial literacy is now measured, and it is low exactly where exclusion is high: among women, rural adults and people with little schooling. That makes it a real enabler and a fair target for policy. But the data shows correlation, and SIM ownership, phones, income and family permission separate the included from the excluded at least as sharply. Literacy belongs among the top enablers, alongside them, not above them.

## What India and Brazil teach, read against Pakistan

**Mass basic accounts reached women in India.** India's Jan Dhan scheme has 59.09 crore accounts, 55.7 percent held by women and 77.8 percent in rural and semi urban areas [S9]. India's Findex gender gap in accounts is not statistically significant [S1, p. 98]. What made it work included a near universal ID. Pakistan has the ID base too, with 89 percent of women holding one [S3, p. 19]. What blocks it here is the SIM gap: a phone led drive misses women without their own SIM [S12, p. 10]. And in India 16 percent of account owners have only inactive accounts [S1, p. 197]. Confidence that the model transfers: medium, if paired with assisted, agent based onboarding.

**Government payments can open accounts at speed (Brazil).** Brazil's emergency aid in the pandemic used digital savings accounts and Pix and reached more than 100 million people in nine months [S1, p. 260]. Pakistan does this at smaller scale: a 2026 Ramzan package sent about PKR 23.2 billion to some 1.79 million families through banks and branchless banking [S11, p. 57]. What helps: BISP's reach and NADRA's ID. What blocks: the phone gap again. Confidence: medium.

**Financial education at scale is slow and structural (Brazil).** Brazil's central bank runs a school program in more than 27,600 schools covering 67 percent of municipalities, and requires financial institutions to keep a financial education policy [S7, p. 109]. Pakistan's curriculum project is at the model chapter stage [S11, p. 48]. Both are long term work, and Brazil started from 86 percent account ownership [S1, p. 295]. Confidence as a near term driver: low.

## What this means for Part 3

Two findings carry forward. Access is the first gap, and it is mostly about women, income, phones and family permission. Use among those inside is already common but thin, and cash still carries most value.

For customers, this means three things. Inclusion has to start with women's own SIMs and accounts. Protection has to work for people who read with difficulty and may be more exposed to fraud [S1, p. 110]. And the experience has to fit basic phones and low literacy, not only apps.

Part 3 looks at open banking as a consumer product. Consent screens, liability rules and complaint routes only empower people who can understand them, including users who share a phone or read with difficulty. Whether that comes from design, financial education or both is the question Part 3 picks up.

## Limits

- Several Findex breakdowns are regional, not for Pakistan [S1].
- Karandaaz publishes its financial literacy results as percentages and index scores; I did not have the microdata to test cause [S12].
- The GSMA barrier ranking comes from a chart [S5].
- SBP FY26 figures are provisional [S2].
- Survey years differ: Findex 2024 [S1], K-FIS 2024 [S12], census 2023 [S6], SBP FY26 [S2] [S11].

I would value feedback and other readings of the data.
