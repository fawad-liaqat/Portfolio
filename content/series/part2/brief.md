# The adoption gap: who is outside, and why

**Part 2 of 4: Pakistan's digital public infrastructure series**

Fawad Liaqat. Evidence as of 17 November 2026. Source markers such as [S1] resolve to the Sources section at the end and to `sources.md`. Table data is in `adoption_data.json`.

## The question

Part 1 found that the payment rail is not the bottleneck. Raast moved 2,755.2 million transactions in FY26 [S2, p. 31], yet only 27 percent of Pakistani adults have a financial account and 25 percent made or received a digital payment [S1, p. 298]. This part asks two things in order. First, who is outside the system and what keeps them out. Second, for people already inside, what keeps cash in their hands. Then it tests one popular explanation, financial literacy, against the data.

## Three numbers that measure different things

Readers will meet very different inclusion figures for Pakistan, so it helps to separate them first.

| Measure | Value | What it counts |
|---|---|---|
| Global Findex 2025 | 27% of adults [S1, p. 298] | Adults 15+ who say they personally hold an account at a bank, similar institution or mobile money provider [S1, p. 83] |
| Karandaaz K-FIS 2024 | 35% of adults; men 56%, women 14% [S12] | Access to a bank, mobile wallet or nonbank financial account, from a survey of 6,624 households [S12] |
| SBP, 2025 | 69% of adults; gender gap 29% [S11, pp. 47 and 48] | Broadly, the share of adults with a bank account, from SBP data [S11, p. 47] |

The State Bank's new Financial Inclusion Index is a different kind of number again: a score of 59.5 for 2025, built from 69 indicators. Its access score is 74.1, usage 68.5 and quality 43.5 [S11, p. 48]. Quality is the weakest part. Survey measures count people. Administrative measures count accounts, and Pakistan has 292.8 million customer accounts and wallets [S2, p. 10] against a population of 241.5 million [S2, p. 9]. One person can hold several. SBP's figure rose from 64 percent in 2023 against a target of 75 percent by 2028 [S15, pp. 1 and 23]. I use Findex for comparisons with other countries and Karandaaz, where readable, for detail inside Pakistan.

## Part A: access. Who is outside?

### Women

Every source agrees on direction. Findex reports a 30 point gender gap in account ownership, four or more times the global gap [S1, p. 98]. Karandaaz finds 14 percent of women included against 56 percent of men [S12]. Even the State Bank, whose measure gives higher numbers, reports a 29 percent gap for 2025, with women at 52 percent at the end of 2025 [S11, p. 48] [S10].

The gap starts before the bank. Findex puts the gap in phone ownership between Pakistani men and women at 65 points, far above the 14 to 25 points it reports for Bangladesh, Egypt, Ethiopia and India [S1, p. 25]. Only 5 percent of men lack a phone [S1, p. 32]. Karandaaz reports 47 percent of women have a SIM registered in their own name, against 78 percent of men, and a registered SIM is needed to open a wallet [S12]. Branchless banking app users (99.1 million) far outnumber bank app users (30.4 million) [S2, p. 9], so a woman without her own SIM is outside the busiest entry door.

### People outside the labour force

Pakistan has the largest gap in South Asia between adults in and out of the labour force, at 29 points [S1, p. 101]. People are paid into accounts at work. Without a wage, there is less reason to open one.

### Poorer and less educated adults

Findex does not print Pakistan's own row for these. At the regional level, 71 percent of South Asian adults without an account have a primary education or less [S1, p. 110]. Across low and middle income economies the education gap in account ownership is 13 points, and Findex notes that less educated adults are also more likely to be poor [S1, p. 101]. Education and income move together, so neither can be read alone.

### Teenagers

A new State Bank framework lets people aged 13 to 18 own and operate accounts and wallets on their own, closing what SBP calls a longstanding gap for about 26 million people [S11, p. 48]. It is too new to measure.

### Rural adults

Census literacy shows the rural divide sharply: 41.67 percent of rural women aged 10 and over are literate, against 78.13 percent of urban men [S6]. Findex reports that across low and middle income economies, rural adults are 16 points less likely to make a digital payment [S1, p. 171]. I could not read a Pakistan rural figure for account ownership.

### What the unbanked say

Findex asked adults without a bank account why. In South Asia, excluding India, 70 percent said they do not have enough money, 36 percent said fees are too high, 24 percent said banks are too far, 20 percent lack documents, 17 percent said a family member has one and 15 percent do not trust institutions [S1, p. 111]. Respondents could pick several. Pakistan's own row is not printed, and financial literacy was not one of the options.

## Part B: use. Why does cash persist for people already inside?

Here the Pakistan data turns a common story around. Among Pakistani account owners, 90 percent or more use digital payments, in both poorer and wealthier households [S1, p. 171]. That is higher than India, where only 54 percent of account owners do [S1, p. 167]. Findex suggests social transfer payments into accounts may drive it [S1, p. 171]. BISP serves more than 10 million beneficiaries, mostly women, and the State Bank has issued instructions to move them to Social Protection Wallets [S11, p. 57]. So most people inside the system already pay digitally at least once a year. The problem is what they pay for and how often.

**Cash still carries value.** Over the counter transactions stayed above 60 percent of retail value in FY26 [S11, p. 21]; my calculation from the review gives about 62 percent (PKR 415.7 of 672.7 trillion) [S2, p. 9]. Currency in circulation grew from PKR 10,634.5 billion to 11,941.5 billion, about 12 percent (my calculation) [S2, p. 9]. Nearly 98 percent of cash deposits are still made at branches and agents [S2, p. 19].

**Acceptance is wide but thin, and it was paid for.** Pakistan has 3.8 million QR merchants, but they took 297 million QR and till payments in a year [S2, pp. 9 and 10], about 77 each (my calculation). Most of them are new: 2.68 million joined Raast in FY26 [S11, p. 58]. A federal subsidy of PKR 3.5 billion paid 0.5 percent of each Raast merchant payment, capped at PKR 100, from September 2025 to June 2026 [S11, pp. 21 and 58]. Monthly merchant payments rose from 4.3 million to 86 million over that period [S11, p. 21]. That June figure is about 24 payments per registered merchant per month (my calculation, 86 million over 3.63 million [S11, p. 58]). The subsidy has now ended. Whether the habit holds without it is the test to watch. Merchant payments are 6.6 percent of Raast volume (my calculation from [S2, p. 31]). In India they are 63 percent of UPI volume, and 86 percent of those are under INR 500 [S8]. Small daily purchases are where India's rail earns its habits.

**Cost and trust.** Fees are the second most cited barrier to accounts in South Asia [S1, p. 111]. On trust, 15 percent of the unbanked in the region cite it [S1, p. 111], and Pakistan still has no data protection law [S3, p. 46]. I found no Pakistan specific survey data on fraud or trust among users.

**Design.** Pakistan has a relatively large share of basic phone owners [S1, p. 20]. India built a channel for them, UPI123Pay [S14]. The State Bank's open banking draft builds its consent journey around app dashboards and app PINs [S4, p. 7].

**Women inside the system.** Findex prints 74 percent of Pakistani women account owners using digital payments, against 95 percent of men [S1, p. 170]. The figure title on the same page says no gap is present in Pakistan, so I treat this one as unsettled.

## Part C: testing the financial literacy hypothesis

The claim worth testing is simple: people stay out, or stay in cash, because they do not understand financial services. Here is what the evidence I could read says.

**Evidence that supports a literacy role.** For Pakistani men and women who know about mobile internet but do not use it, difficulty reading or writing is the top single reason in GSMA's 2024 survey [S5, pp. 66 to 67]. Findex lists difficulty reading or typing as a common barrier to phone ownership in South Asia [S1, p. 32]. South Asian women without a bank account are at least 19 points more likely than men to say they would need help using one [S1, p. 246]. Karandaaz reports a self rated digital ability score of 48 for women against 65 for men [S13].

**Evidence that points elsewhere.** Lack of money and fees top the barrier list [S1, p. 111]. Family disapproval is cited by 40 percent of Pakistani women without a phone, and by no men [S1, p. 32]. And once inside, 90 percent or more of Pakistani account owners use digital payments regardless of income [S1, p. 171]. Findex itself warns that financial education helps but is not enough alone, and that many problems come from pricing, design and opacity rather than user mistakes [S1, p. 239].

**What policy is already doing.** The State Bank's financial education roadmap reached 2.6 million people in FY26, and work to put financial literacy into the school curriculum has finalised 192 learning outcomes for grades 1 to 12 [S11, p. 48]. SBP credits financial literacy, among other things, for rising index scores [S11, p. 48]. That is an assessment, not a test.

**What the data cannot settle.** None of the sources I read measures financial literacy in Pakistan directly. The State Bank's own strategy plans a new demand side survey that would capture the level of financial literacy [S15, p. 19]. Its last such surveys were in 2008 and 2015 [S15, p. 4]. The evidence above is about reading, digital skills or confidence. I could not read a Pakistan table of account ownership by education or literacy. Karandaaz publishes Raast awareness by education level, but the values sit behind a login, which I did not use.

**My reading.** Basic literacy and digital skills look like a real barrier to the phone and internet channel, mainly for women. Financial literacy as such is untested. Income, cost, family permission and phone ownership have at least as much support. Literacy belongs on the list of enablers, not at the top of it.

## What India and Brazil teach, read against Pakistan

**Mass basic accounts reached women in India.** India's Jan Dhan scheme has 59.09 crore accounts, 55.7 percent held by women and 77.8 percent in rural and semi urban areas [S9]. India's Findex gender gap in accounts is not statistically significant [S1, p. 98]. What made it work included a near universal ID. Pakistan has the ID base too, with 89 percent of women holding one [S3, p. 19]. What blocks it here is the phone and SIM gap: a phone led drive misses women without their own SIM [S12]. A caution travels as well. In India 16 percent of account owners have only inactive accounts [S1, p. 197]. Confidence that the model transfers: medium, if paired with assisted, agent based onboarding.

**Government payments can open accounts at speed (Brazil).** Brazil's emergency aid in the pandemic used digital savings accounts and Pix and reached more than 100 million people in nine months [S1, p. 260]. Pakistan already pays relief this way at smaller scale: a 2026 Ramzan package sent about PKR 23.2 billion to some 1.79 million families through banks and branchless banking [S11, p. 57]. What helps: BISP's reach and NADRA's ID. What blocks: the phone gap again. Confidence: medium.

**Financial education at scale is slow and structural (Brazil).** Brazil's central bank runs a school program in more than 27,600 schools covering 67 percent of municipalities, and requires financial institutions to keep a financial education policy [S7, p. 109]. Pakistan's curriculum project is at the model chapter stage [S11, p. 48]. Both are long term complements, not quick fixes. Brazil already had 86 percent account ownership [S1, p. 295]. Confidence that it transfers as a driver of near term adoption: low.

**Small merchant payments build habits (India).** Most UPI payments go to merchants and most are small [S8]. Pakistan's merchant share is far lower [S2, p. 31]. Confidence: medium on direction, low on mechanism, since I found no primary source on why Indian merchants adopted.

## What this means for Part 3

Two findings carry forward. Access is the first gap, and it is mostly about women, income, phones and family permission. Use among those inside is already common but thin, and cash still carries most value.

Part 3 looks at open banking as a consumer product. If consent screens, liability rules and complaint routes are to empower people, they have to be understood by users who may share a phone, may read with difficulty and may need help. Whether that understanding comes from design, from financial education or from both is the question Part 3 picks up.

## Limits

- Findex prints few Pakistan specific breakdowns; several are regional [S1].
- Karandaaz figures are from press reports, including one by Karandaaz staff, not the report itself [S12] [S13].
- The GSMA barrier ranking was read from a chart by an automated reader [S5].
- SBP FY26 figures are provisional [S2].
- Survey years differ: Findex 2024 [S1], K-FIS 2024 [S12], census 2023 [S6], SBP FY26 [S2] [S11].

I would value feedback and other readings of the data.
