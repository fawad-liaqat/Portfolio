# Where digital payments earn their keep: a use case casebook

**Part 4 of 4: Pakistan's digital public infrastructure series**

Fawad Liaqat. Evidence as of 17 November 2026. Source markers such as [S1] resolve to the Sources section at the end and to `sources.md`. Case data is in `casebook.json`.

## The question

Parts 1 and 2 found that Pakistan's payment rail works and that the harder problem is getting people onto it and giving them reasons to use it. Raast moved 2,755.2 million transactions in FY26 [S2, p. 31], while 27 percent of adults hold an account [S1, p. 298]. This part asks a practical question. Where, in the world and in Pakistan, have digital payments clearly beaten cash for a specific job? For each of six verticals I take one documented case, the evidence that it delivered value, the conditions that made it work, and what it would take here.

A note on the bar. A case counts only if a primary or official source documents both adoption and some benefit. Where I could find adoption but not benefit, I say so.

## 1. Government to person transfers

**The case: Brazil's emergency aid.** During the pandemic Brazil paid its emergency aid through free digital social savings accounts and Pix transfers, and reached more than 100 million people in nine months [S1, p. 260]. Findex notes that speed was part of the reason countries chose digital accounts [S1, p. 260].

**Conditions.** A free account created for the purpose, and an instant payment rail people could use to move the money on. Pix today is used by 172 million people and 22.2 million businesses [S8, p. 93].

**Pakistan.** The World Bank counts Pakistan among countries that used digital databases and data matching to reach, on average, 51 percent of their population with pandemic support [S4, p. 73]. The pieces have grown since. BISP serves over 10 million beneficiaries, mostly women, and the State Bank has issued instructions to move them from the old limited mandate accounts to Social Protection Wallets [S3, p. 57]. The 2026 Ramzan package paid about PKR 23.2 billion to some 1.79 million families through banks and branchless banking, usable for merchant payments and bills or cash out at agents after biometric checks [S3, pp. 56 to 57]. A fuel subsidy of PKR 13.3 billion went to accounts and wallets using data from the Pakistan Digital Authority [S3, p. 57].

**What it would take.** Wallets that are free to hold, open on any phone, and accept the transfer without a visit to an agent. The phone gap from Part 2 is the limit: a wallet transfer reaches a woman only if she controls a phone or an agent nearby [S1, p. 25]. Confidence that this transfers: high. It is already happening.

## 2. Merchant payments

**The case: India's UPI at the shop counter.** Person to merchant payments are 63 percent of UPI volume, and 86 percent of them are below INR 500 [S5]. The system handled about 24,162 crore transactions in FY 2025 to 26 with 741 banks live by July 2026 [S5].

**Evidence of value.** The official release documents scale and small ticket use, which shows people pay for everyday goods this way [S5]. It does not measure cost savings for merchants. I found no primary source on why Indian merchants adopted, so the mechanism stays open.

**Pakistan.** The State Bank and the federal government ran a natural experiment. A PKR 3.5 billion subsidy paid 0.5 percent of each Raast merchant payment, capped at PKR 100, from September 2025 to June 2026 [S3, pp. 21 and 58]. Monthly merchant payments rose from 4.3 million to 86 million [S3, p. 21]. Registered merchants reached 3.63 million, with 2.68 million joining in FY26 [S3, p. 58]. Banks must now give every new business account at least one digital payment method [S3, p. 55].

**What it would take.** Use that survives the end of the subsidy in June 2026 [S3, p. 58]. The test is the data from July 2026 onward, which I could not find yet. Confidence: medium on direction, low on durability.

## 3. Remittances

**The case: Pakistan's own inbound remittances.** About 92 percent of home remittances are now credited directly to accounts or wallets [S3, p. 55]. The State Bank has allowed account to account credit of remittances through channels such as Raast [S3, p. 58]. Egypt shows the same pattern: its instant payment network lets banks credit inward remittances to accounts around the clock [S6, p. 43].

**Evidence of value.** Findex cites research that having an account to receive payments increases the frequency and volume of remittances from family and friends [S1, p. 165]. The State Bank links direct crediting to lower cost receipt through formal channels [S3, p. 55]. I found no Pakistan figure for the cost saved.

**Conditions.** A sender abroad who already uses a formal channel, and a receiving account at home. The sender side does the onboarding.

**What it would take.** Turning the remittance account into a used account. A family that receives money digitally can pay bills and shops digitally, if the shop accepts it. Confidence: high that the credit side works; medium that it leads to wider use.

## 4. Utility and bill payments

**The case: Kenya.** Close to 100 percent of Kenyan adults who paid utility bills did so digitally [S1, p. 179]. Across low and middle income economies the share rose from 32 to 39 percent of utility bill payers between 2014 and 2024 [S1, p. 179].

**Evidence of value.** Findex points out that bills come due on a regular cadence, which builds habit, and that paying digitally can improve on time payment [S1, p. 179]. Those are stated as general findings, not measured for Kenya alone.

**Pakistan.** This is already a quiet domestic success. Digital channels processed 89 percent of bill payments and mobile top ups in FY26, up from 85 percent in FY24 [S2, p. 18]. That counts transactions through formal channels, not people. Findex shows that across South Asia only about a third of utility payers pay digitally [S1, p. 179], so many households still pay in cash outside the channels the State Bank counts.

**What it would take.** Reaching households that pay at a shop or agent in cash. Confidence that Pakistan can build on it: high.

## 5. Agriculture and supply chains

**The case: Kenya.** In Kenya, 71 percent of people who received payments for agricultural products were paid digitally, mostly into mobile money accounts on a phone [S1, p. 187]. Across low and middle income economies, only about a quarter of such payments go into accounts [S1, p. 186].

**Evidence of value.** Findex notes that accounts help farmers save across seasons and finance inputs [S1, p. 186]. That is a general benefit, not a measured Kenyan outcome.

**Pakistan.** The cattle market campaign is the clearest local case. In Eid ul Azha 2026, QR and biometric account opening covered 123 cattle markets, with about 481,000 transactions worth PKR 34 billion and about 12,500 new accounts, up from 54 markets and PKR 4.7 billion a year earlier [S2, p. 21]. The State Bank itself notes that lasting impact depends on continued use after the season [S2, p. 21]. The new Zarkhez-e platform offers fully digital agricultural loans [S3, p. 48]; no outcome data yet.

**What it would take.** Paying farmers digitally at the point of sale through commission agents and mandis, not just at a festival. Confidence: medium.

## 6. Education and health fees

**The case: Côte d'Ivoire's school fees.** From a 2011 pilot, the education ministry moved secondary school registration fees to digital channels. By 2014 to 2015, 99 percent of 1.48 million payments were digital and 94 percent were by mobile money [S7, p. 10]. The case study reports sharp falls in lost payments, fraud and theft, earlier collection of funds and better student records [S7, pp. 18 to 19]. Disputed transactions at one provider fell from 12 percent to 1 percent between 2012 and 2014 [S7, p. 10]. Students paid no transaction fee; the ministry paid providers a flat fee per payment [S7, p. 11]. This case dates from 2015.

**Conditions.** One payee, the ministry, setting the rule; a single deadline; a known amount; and the payer spared the fee. September brings nearly 80 percent of payments, which stretched agent cash [S7, p. 15].

**Pakistan.** Raast QR acceptance has spread to educational and healthcare facilities and public service points, and QR is now the only way to pay at NADRA centres [S3, p. 58]. I found no figures on fee volumes at schools or hospitals.

**What it would take.** A provincial education department or a large hospital network setting digital fees as the default, with zero cost to the payer. Confidence: medium. The model fits Pakistan's institutions, but there is no local data yet.

## What the six cases have in common

Read together, the cases show a pattern.

1. **An institution sets the default.** A government, a ministry or a regulator decides that money arrives or must be paid digitally: Brazil's aid [S1, p. 260], Côte d'Ivoire's fees [S7, p. 10], NADRA's counters [S3, p. 58].
2. **The payment repeats.** Bills, school terms and transfers come on a schedule [S1, p. 179].
3. **Cash is costly or risky.** Theft at fee collection [S7, p. 18] and large cash sums at cattle markets [S2, p. 21].
4. **The payer pays nothing extra.** Free accounts in Brazil [S1, p. 260], no fee for Ivorian students [S7, p. 11], subsidised Raast merchant payments [S3, p. 58].

Everyday merchant payments are the one vertical where none of these hold naturally, which is why they took a subsidy in Pakistan.

## Series synthesis

**Part 1** asked how complete Pakistan's digital public infrastructure is. The payment rail holds up against India, Brazil and Egypt. Digital ID is a pilot, the data sharing layer is a draft, and there is no data protection law.

**Part 2** asked who is outside and why. Access is the first gap, led by women, income, phones and family permission. Once inside, most account owners do pay digitally [S1, p. 171]. Financial literacy is one enabler among several, and Pakistan has no direct measure of it.

**Part 3** looks at the State Bank's open banking draft as a consumer product: whether people can understand and control what they consent to.

**Part 4** shows where digital payments beat cash when the conditions line up. For Pakistan, the near term wins look like government transfers into wallets, fees paid to the state and its institutions, bills, and remittances. Everyday shop payments are the harder habit to change. The common thread across the series is that the rails are no longer the constraint. What matters now is who can get on, what they can do once there, and whether the system is built for the phone and the skills people actually have.

## Limits

- Several cases measure adoption well and benefit only in general terms [S1] [S5].
- The Côte d'Ivoire case is from 2015 [S7].
- Pakistan's FY26 figures are provisional [S2].
- No post subsidy data on Raast merchant payments was available [S3].
- Kenya figures are survey shares of adults, while Pakistan's bill figure counts transactions [S1, p. 179] [S2, p. 18].

I would value feedback and other readings of the data.
