# Open banking in Pakistan: what you would be agreeing to

Your bank holds a record of your money: your balance and every payment in and out. Open banking lets you tell your bank to share that record, or to make a payment, through another licensed app. The State Bank of Pakistan has published draft rules for this [S1].

**An everyday example.** Sana runs a small clothing shop and wants a loan. Today she would collect stamped bank statements. Under the draft, she opens a licensed lending app, chooses her bank, and agrees to share six months of transactions for one purpose. The app checks her identity through NADRA's biometric system, her bank sends a one time password to her phone, and she approves inside her bank's app with her PIN [S1]. The lender sees her history and decides.

**What the draft promises Sana.**

- She must be told what is shared, why, and for how long, before she agrees [S1].
- No app may ever ask for her bank PIN or password [S1].
- She can cancel at any time from a dashboard, and the app must delete her data within 30 days [S1].
- If money leaves her account without her approval, her bank must pay her back within seven business days of a valid claim, then settle with the app [S1].
- If she is refused a loan because of a score from such an app, the lender must tell her and name the app [S1].
- She can complain by phone, email, chat or at a branch [S1].

**What it assumes.** Sana has an account, a smartphone with two apps, and can read a dashboard. Many Pakistanis do not. Only 27 in 100 adults have an account, and women are far less likely than men to own a phone [S5]. The draft lets people complain by phone, but cancelling a consent is online only [S1].

**How others did it.** The UK started in 2017 by ordering its nine largest banks to open up, and had about 16.6 million connected users by late 2025 [S11] [S10] [S8]. Brazil's central bank reached over 154 million active consents by the end of 2025 [S7]. India's licensed aggregators had linked 352 million accounts by September 2026 [S15].

**Why it matters.** Rights on paper help only people who know them and can use them. A cancel option by SMS or call, consent summaries in Urdu, and public monthly figures would help more people use these rights.
